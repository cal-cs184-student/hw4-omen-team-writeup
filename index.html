Part 1: Masses and springs

I represented the cloth as a two-dimensional grid of point masses stored in row-major order. Depending on the selected orientation, the masses were positioned in either the xz-plane or the xy-plane. I then connected neighboring masses with three types of springs: structural springs between adjacent horizontal and vertical masses, shearing springs between diagonal neighbors, and bending springs between masses separated by two grid positions.

The following images compare the cloth wireframe under different spring constraint configurations. Structural and bending constraints produce the horizontal and vertical grid structure, while shearing constraints create diagonal connections between neighboring point masses.

Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

For each simulation step, I reset the force on every point mass and accumulated external forces using Newton's second law. I then applied Hooke's law to every enabled spring, using a reduced spring constant for bending springs. I updated each unpinned point mass with Verlet integration and included the damping term to reduce its previous displacement. Finally, I applied a deformation constraint that prevents any spring from stretching beyond 110% of its rest length. When one endpoint is pinned, the entire correction is applied to the unpinned endpoint; otherwise, the correction is divided evenly between the two masses.

Effect of the spring constant

With a low spring constant of \(k_s = 500\), the springs exert weaker restoring forces, so the cloth is more flexible and exhibits greater sagging and deformation. At \(k_s = 50000\), the much stronger restoring forces resist stretching and make the cloth appear stiffer and smoother as it falls.

Effect of density

With density set to \(1.5\,\mathrm{g/cm^2}\), the cloth has less mass and the springs resist its weight more effectively. At \(150\,\mathrm{g/cm^2}\), gravity produces a much larger load relative to the spring forces, resulting in more pronounced sagging and curvature. Both tests used \(k_s = 5000\) and approximately 0.2% damping.

Effect of damping

Damping primarily changes the transient motion rather than the final equilibrium shape. With 0% damping, the cloth retains its energy and continues swinging, folding, and oscillating. The default damping of approximately 0.2% removes a small amount of energy each step. At 1% damping, energy dissipates more quickly, so the motion is more controlled and the cloth settles sooner.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

I implemented collisions against spheres and planes by testing every point mass against every collision object during each simulation step. For a sphere, a point mass inside the radius is projected to the nearest point on the sphere's surface. The correction is measured from the mass's previous position and scaled by 1 - friction, which allows friction to reduce its tangential motion.

For a plane, I compare the signed distances of the point mass's previous and current positions from the plane. Opposite signs indicate that its trajectory crossed the plane. I compute the intersection point along that trajectory, move the mass slightly back to its original side using SURFACE_OFFSET, and again scale the correction by 1 - friction. This small offset prevents the cloth from repeatedly intersecting the plane because of floating-point error.

Cloth colliding with a sphere

The following images show the cloth at rest on the sphere for three spring constants. With \(k_s = 500\), the weak restoring forces allow the cloth to stretch farther and form longer, narrower folds. At the default \(k_s = 5000\), the deformation is more moderate. With \(k_s = 50000\), the cloth resists stretching much more strongly, producing a broader and stiffer drape with less vertical elongation.
